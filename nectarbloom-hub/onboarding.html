Orientation
Orientation

Two queues, one item

An evaluation item is built in Queue 1, then run against models in Queue 2. Most contributors work in one queue, but the handoff is what the work is for.

Queue 1Prompt and Rubrics

You build the item: the opening prompt, the target deliverables, the GTFA, the weighted rubric and, on Type 2, the Known / Not Known steering script.

Output: a finished, frozen item.

Queue 2SxS

You run that item as a full 20+ turn conversation against two models, grade both against the existing rubric, rate eight dimensions and record one preference.

Output: comparable preference data.

The rule that spans both queues: every file, person, date and event you reference must actually exist in your hydrated universe. Never invent an entity; never mock an input file.

Phase 0 · Account

Two links, and picking the wrong one is the classic mistake

Two accounts are in play. You run the models in a Gemini demo account you claim; the item's universe lives in a hydrated Gmail address you create and keep for the whole project. These two links manage that second one.

/setup · Rare

Changes whose life is in the account.

  • Your very first account
  • A different persona or universe
  • Same address, never make a second Gmail

/reset · Constant

Puts that same life back to how it started.

  • Before every new task
  • Before each model's run
  • Before a Type 2 simplified trajectory
Reset before a run, never after one. Everything the model produced is evidence you still need: files to upload, drafts to check, events to verify while grading.  ·  synthetic-account-hydration.outlier.ai/setup · /reset

Getting set up: do these once, in this order

About ten minutes, most of it waiting. Steps 2 to 6 repeat each time you are moved to a new persona or universe: same Gmail address, new selection in the dropdown.

  1. 1
    Claim your Gemini demo account

    app.outlier.ai/demo-account-claim. The account you run models in, and the one your login and model-selector screenshots must show.

  2. 2
    Create the Gmail address, once

    PersonaFirstName.YourConvention@gmail.com. Never a personal account. Ask your QM which universe to hydrate.

  3. 3
    Open /setup and enter your details

    Pick your universe from the Task workspace dropdown, type the exact address, click Continue with Google. Be signed in to outlier.ai first.

  4. 4
    Click through the "hasn't verified this app" warning

    Expected: the hydrator is an internal tool. Advanced, then Go to outlier.ai (unsafe). Some accounts skip this screen.

  5. 5
    Grant all three permissions

    Tick Select all: Gmail, Drive and Calendar. Partial permissions hydrate mail but no Drive, which is painful to diagnose.

  6. 6
    Wait for hydration to finish, up to five minutes

    Watch the new inbox fill in another tab. Do not start a task until the account genuinely looks lived-in.

The hydrator never asks for your Google password. If any Outlier page does, stop and raise it in the War Room.

Phase 0 · Discipline

When to reset, and when not to

Reset

  • Before starting a new task
  • Before the first model's run
  • Before the second model's run
  • Before a Type 2 simplified trajectory

Do not reset

  • Midway through a run
  • After a run, before uploading files
  • Moving to a new persona (use /setup instead)
Why the second reset matters most. Models take real actions: they create events, save files and leave drafts, and those persist. Run a second model without resetting and it starts in a world the first one rearranged, so the comparison is worthless. Wait for the green "Your workspace is restored" before you open a chat.

Queue 1 · The craft

The opening prompt is deliberately incomplete

The gap between what the opener states and what it withholds is the difficulty of your item. An item that gives everything away in Turn 1 cannot produce a 20+ turn conversation.

The rules that matter most

  • A partial request: 2-3 loose asks, not the full task
  • Start abstract; let the goal emerge over the conversation
  • Lead with motive and situation, never a task list
  • Ground every entity in your universe
  • Leave room for judgment, not a mechanical fetch
  • Natural, human, written by you: no LLM, paste is disabled
  • Native register for your locale, never a translation
  • Deliverables live in a separate field, never in the opener
Good opener
"My quarterly numbers are due to the board Friday and something feels off in the March figures, can you help me get to the bottom of it before then?"
Real stakes. No artifact, format or tool named.
Too easy, rewrite
"Create a one-page PDF of my March expenses with a totals row and email it to my manager."
Names the artifact, format and action. A strong model one-shots it.

Queue 1 · The answer key

Target deliverables, then the GTFA

Work the task live first, so the outcome reflects what a strong result actually is. Then write it down. Anything you leave out cannot be graded later.

Every deliverable covers three things

  • The named artifacts, with the specific values and sections that must appear
  • The named side effects: drafts, calendar entries, connector actions, with recipients and timing
  • What must be absent: out-of-scope data, other personas' PII, a draft that was sent

The GTFA covers all four

  • The concrete deliverables
  • The correct values and figures
  • The side effects: events created, drafts prepared, files produced
  • What must be absent: no hallucinated contacts, no unauthorised actions
Write deliverables as final states, never as predicted user moves. "The user might ask to fix the budget sheet" cannot be graded. Every exact value in a criterion must trace back to the GTFA or the deliverables.

Queue 1 · Type 2
Queue 1 · Type 2 only

The simplified trajectory and the 40% gate

A complexity test of your item, not an evaluation of the model. You deliberately give the model the easiest possible path, all deliverables in 1-3 turns, no steering, and check that it still fails most of your rubric.

≤ 40%of the available rubric weight may pass, or the item is too easy.
  1. 1
    Rehydrate first

    The gate is only meaningful against a clean environment.

  2. 2
    1-3 turns, unsteered

    If you catch yourself nudging the model, stop and start over.

  3. 3
    Save the share link after every turn

    Each turn has its own URL and cannot be recovered.

  4. 4
    Grade against the same rubric

    Not a separate, easier one.

Fix the item, not the measurement. Do not delete or weaken criteria to force the number down. Add constraints, edge cases, cross-referencing and multi-artifact coordination, then re-run and re-grade. The percentage is weighted: failing a +3 criterion moves it far more than a +1.

Queue 1 · The rubric

A rubric is a list of checkable facts

Not a scoring guide, not guidelines, not a description of what good looks like. Specific statements about the work, each true or false when you look at it.

A criterion
"The report includes a section titled Risks."
True or false, and everyone who looks will agree.
Not a criterion
"The report should be well structured and easy to follow."
This is a hope. Two readers will not mark it the same way.

Repeatability

The same work scores the same from anyone.

Comparability

Two runs against one fixed standard, the product.

A record

Point at the criterion, not at taste.

Speed

Checking facts beats forming an opinion.

Binary, never scaled. Avoid "Rate the clarity 1 to 5" (every reader picks a different number). Prefer "The summary is 200 words or fewer" (one reader, one answer). Wanting a scale usually means two or three binary criteria are hiding in one line.

Queue 1
Queue 1 · The rubric

10-30 criteria, each weighted +1 to +3

Every criterion must be

  • Binary: unambiguously pass or fail
  • Observable in the artifact or transcript, never in reasoning
  • Product-independent: no product name, no interface quirk
  • Atomic: one requirement each, no "and" chains
  • Positive: States / Includes / Reports / Provides
  • Self-contained: decidable with only the prompt and input files

Weighting

  • +3 Essential: failure means the user's goal is not completed
  • +2 Important: the output is unusable as delivered without it
  • +1 Minor positive: a nice-to-have; the task is still fulfilled
Run the "looks-correct-but-wrong" test on every rubric. Describe one output that would look plausible yet should fail: right numbers but the draft was sent; a figure from a stale source; a fabricated name that reads real. If your rubric would not catch it, add the criterion that does. Weight inflation is the common failure: if most of your list is +3, the weights distinguish nothing.

Queue 1 · Order of work

Work the task before you write a single criterion

The order is fixed, and it is the part most often skipped. You cannot write a rubric against an imagined result.

  1. 1
    First: define the intent and opening prompt

    The concrete deliverable a real user would pay for.

  2. 2
    Then: run the task live, on more than one model

    Several turns. You are looking for how the task actually behaves.

  3. 3
    Then: fix the target outcome

    The reference final state. This becomes the anchor for everything scored.

  4. 4
    Only then: write the criteria against it

    The target outcome is what makes a criterion checkable.

The person applying your rubric has never seen your reasoning. Every value, filename, recipient and date should trace to the target outcome and GTFA. "Reports the net distribution as $7,626.60" is only writable because you worked the task and know the number.

Queue 1 · What reviewers find

Where rubrics actually fail

Across 297 criteria flagged as defective, the distribution was lopsided. The two at the top are more than half of everything found, and both are caught by one question.

Ask one question of every line"Would a perfect, honest response pass this, and would two people reading only the prompt agree on borderline work?"

Duplication and bundling are what people instinctively proofread for, and they are almost never the problem.

Review thresholds: roughly 10% of criteria carrying a major defect, 15% major-or-moderate, or 20% carrying any defect, and the rubric fails review.

Queue 1 · The rubric

The two defects that cost the most

Together they are 56% of everything reviewers find. Both are caught by asking one question of each line. Every example below is a real submitted criterion.

26%  Punishes the right answer

The criterion rewards something the conversation ruled out, or invents a constraint the user never set. A flawless response fails it.

"Provides the file already in the first turn."
At turn 3 the user said to produce the document only on request.

Test: would a perfect, honest response pass this?

30%  Wording two people cannot apply

The criterion turns on a word with no observable feature, so the result depends on who is scoring.

"Maintains a reassuring tone in the note."
Tone has no threshold, and this note also delivers bad news.

Test: would two people agree on borderline work?

The fix is always the same move: replace the adjective with the observable feature. "Reassuring tone" becomes the specific sentence the note must contain. "Few screenshots" becomes a count.

Queue 1 · The rubric

A worked example

The request: "Pull our Q3 numbers together into something I can send the board on Monday. Keep it short, and flag anything that looks off."

First attempt, none of these work

  • The memo is board-ready. (the whole judgement restated)
  • The memo is short and well written. (bundled, neither half checkable)
  • Correctly identifies the Q3 anomalies. (needs information nobody has)

Same request, checkable

  • Provides a document of 500 words or fewer
  • Reports Q3 revenue as $4.18M
  • States that the September figure is provisional
  • Includes sections titled Summary, Q3 performance and Risks
  • Attaches the document as a file rather than pasting it
Notice what happened to the vague words. Short became a word count. Anything that looks off became the one specific thing that was off. Board-ready dissolved into structure, length and format, and no meaning was lost.

Queue 1 · The rubric

The pass to run before you submit

Rubric creation is independently reviewed, so run this first. Work down the list once, in order.

Line by line

  • Would a perfect, honest response pass this?
  • Would two people reading only the prompt agree?
  • Decidable from the prompt, environment and delivered work alone?
  • About the delivered state, not how the model got there?
  • Names only entities that exist in the universe?
  • One requirement, present tense, positive, product-neutral?

Once, over the whole list

  • Search for [[ : no placeholder survived
  • Paste-check filenames, values and thresholds, including the direction of every inequality
  • Read it as a set: does any pair contradict, or always pass together?
  • Walk the prompt's asks and tick each against its criterion
  • Between 10 and 30 criteria, every weight 1, 2 or 3
  • Sorted by weight, does the order match what matters to this persona?
The one question, asked of every line: could a stranger, holding only the original request and the delivered work, mark this true or false and get the same answer I would?

Queue 2 · SxS

First question: base task or follow-on?

Every task pairs one item against two models. One side is always the base model, Gemini; the other is a comparison model that changes from task to task.

Base taskGemini vs. Model X

You generate both sides: full trajectory, grading, annotation and ratings for each. You reset twice, once before each model's run.

Reuses nothing.

Follow-on taskGemini vs. a new Model X

You generate the new model only. Gemini's trajectory, grades, ratings and links are reused exactly as produced on the base task.

Reuses the base side.

Never touch an existing trajectory. On a follow-on the Gemini side is final: do not re-run, re-grade, re-rate, re-upload, edit or delete it. Two Geminis in one task is the biggest trap; match the version string character for character and screenshot the selector before Turn 1.

Queue 2 · Steering

Running the trajectory

20+turns is the design target.
< 7turns fails outright.

Good steering

  • Introduce withheld details as a real user would
  • Correct the model, pivot, follow up
  • Keep going when it struggles, struggling is signal
  • Pursue the same deliverables with both models

Bad steering

  • Dumping the whole deliverable list in one turn
  • Nudging the model toward the right answer
  • Padding with re-confirmations, these do not count
  • Abandoning the run because the model is doing badly
Never copy the browser address bar. Always use the product's Share feature. Gemini-family runs need one link per turn; GPT and Claude need only the final link.

Queue 2 · Scoring

Grade, annotate, rate, prefer

Grade

Pass 1 / fail 0 against the frozen rubric. Pass only on evidence in the produced output or a verified side effect. A described intention is a fail; partial satisfaction is a fail.

Annotate

Link every criterion to the turn where it was decided. Passed: the turn that best demonstrates it. Failed: the turn where it is clearest. Never default to Turn 1.

Rate

Eight dimensions, 1-5, with evidence. 200+ character justification on every score; cite turns on every 1-4. Safety is Yes/No, not 1-5.

Prefer

One 1-7 judgement per task. 4 is a tie; 1 and 7 are "much better". Read the slot labels, the order is not fixed. Must agree with your grades and ratings.

Exactly one key turn per model, and it is never Turn 1. There is no 40% gate here: grade honestly. 0% and 100% are both legitimate.

The eval sandbox: check your own work before you submit

A small IDE with Claude Code inside it. You never open a file or type a task ID; the step labels tell you which command is due and when.

CommandQueueBlockingWhat it looks for
/itemQ1BlockingPhantom entities, front-loaded prompts, ungradable wording
/rubricQ1NoWrites the rubric for you from the outcomes and GTFA
/trajectory aQ2BlockingTurn count, gaps, missing or duplicated links, padding
/grading aQ2NoWrites the met / not-met grades with turn anchors
/dimensions aQ2BlockingWrong turns cited, filler justifications, mismatched ratings
/sxs abQ2BlockingDirection flips, understated strength, contradictions
Snapshot, then Save, every time. It grades to the lowest score, not the average. Until you press both, the result lives only in the sandbox. Scale: 5 PASS · 3 NON-FAIL · 2 FAIL · 1 UNAUDITABLE; one finding at 2 fails the whole command.

Before you submit

The checks that fail a task outright

No matter how good the rest of the work is.

  • A deleted chat, it kills every share link at once
  • Turn 1 selected as the key turn
  • Final trajectory link missing, or not a valid public share page
  • An uploaded PDF that contradicts the live share page
  • A browser address-bar URL pasted instead of a Share link
  • An existing trajectory modified, re-run or deleted
  • Fewer than 7 turns in a Queue 2 run
  • An ungrounded entity: a file, person or date not in the universe
  • A named file claimed in the response with no matching upload
  • An LLM-written opening prompt

When in doubt

Ask in the War Room before you run, not after. A wrong account, a wrong model or a wrong universe cannot be salvaged once the run has started, and a question costs far less than a rejected task.
